# Articulate — Story Bible

## The frame

You are the newest junior analyst at **Whetstone & Company**, a small buy-side
shop on the fourth floor of a building whose elevator predates the firm.
Whetstone runs concentrated money for a handful of patient clients. It has no
marketing department, no Bloomberg terminal newer than the intern, and one
non-negotiable rule, painted (badly) on the wall of the conference room:
**"We read the filings."**

The firm was founded thirty years ago by Frank Whetstone, who is mostly
retired and entirely unimpressed by consensus. The desk is run day to day by
Marion Cho, the senior analyst, who hired you. Your training is her problem,
and she treats it the way she treats everything: methodically, dryly, and with
the assumption that you would rather be good at this than be flattered.

The game's five levels are your first five weeks on the desk. Each week Marion
gives you the work a real junior would get, in the order a real junior would
get it: first the alphabet, then the grammar, then the judgement.

- **Week One — Orientation (L1).** Marion hands you a stack of 10-Ks and a
  highlighter. Before you are allowed opinions, you learn to classify any line
  item on sight, read a journal entry like a sentence, and keep the accounting
  equation in your head without moving your lips.
- **Week Two — Tie-outs (L2).** The firm's model of a holding stopped
  balancing over the weekend. You learn that the three statements are one
  system: retained earnings rolls forward, depreciation flows back into CFO,
  and cash ties out or somebody is wrong.
- **Week Three — House Rules (L3).** Two companies in the same business,
  different accounting policies, different reported earnings, same economics.
  Marion wants a memo on which numbers are choices and which are facts.
- **Week Four — The Comp Sheet (L4).** Real filings. You build the comparison
  sheet for a peer group and learn that a number means nothing until it stands
  next to another number — and that a grocer, an airline, and a software
  company are recognizable from their silhouettes alone.
- **Week Five — The Sable File (L5).** A holding, Sable Peak Beverages,
  reports a quarter that Marion describes as "fine, which is the problem."
  Revenue up, receivables up faster, cash flat. You work the file, name the
  technique, cite the evidence, and get five questions with the CFO. This is
  no longer practice.

The frame stays light. There are no cutscenes, no villains twirling anything,
and the stakes are the real ones: the clients' money and your own competence.

## Character sheets

**You (the player).** A junior analyst, background unspecified, smart enough
to be hired and green enough to be dangerous. The game never gives you
dialogue; your answers are your dialogue. Marion's opinion of you improves
exactly as fast as your mastery scores do, and no faster.

**Marion Cho — Senior Analyst, your mentor.** Twenty-four years at Whetstone,
before that four years auditing companies she now shorts on principle. Sharp,
economical, seen every trick in Schilit twice. She delivers all feedback and
hints in the game. Her praise is understated ("that ties"), her corrections
are diagnostic, never cruel, and she cannot say a number is wrong without
telling you which identity or definition would have caught it. Believes
accounting is a language and fluency is a habit. Drinks her coffee black
because washing the milk spoon is a waste of a spoon.

**Frank Whetstone — Founder.** Semi-retired, appears rarely, mostly in level
outros. Talks in aphorisms that turn out to be load-bearing ("cash is a fact,
profit is an opinion"). Started the firm after a stock he owned restated three
years of earnings; has never fully gotten over it, which is the firm's entire
edge.

**Gus Ferreira — Operations, trading, and the coffee machine.** Twenty years
on the desk. Not an accountant and proud of it; his job in the story is to ask
the naive question ("so if they made forty million, why is there no money?")
that the player must be able to answer in plain English. The Translate drills
are, canonically, you explaining things to Gus.

## Tone rules for all in-game text

1. **Second person, present tense.** "You post the entry." Never "the player."
2. **Marion's voice for all feedback, hints, and narration.** Dry, precise,
   workplace-real. She is on your side; she is just not going to pretend.
3. **No exclamation marks.** Anywhere. Enthusiasm at Whetstone is expressed by
   continued employment.
4. **Every feedback line teaches.** A wrong answer names the concept,
   identity, or definition the player missed — never just "incorrect," and
   never the bare value. A right answer still earns the "so what."
5. **Every explanation ends at the investor.** Why does someone deciding
   whether to own this company care. If an explanation has no "so what," it
   is not finished.
6. **Plain English is the deliverable.** Jargon may appear, but it gets
   translated in the same breath the first time it is used.
7. **Dry wit is seasoning, not the meal.** One understated joke per item at
   most, and never inside the part that teaches.
8. **Understatement over drama.** Nothing "shocking," nothing "massive."
   Numbers are allowed to be alarming on their own.
9. **No gamey fantasy.** No XP-speak, no "quests," no "heroes." It is a desk,
   it is a job, and being good at it is the reward.
